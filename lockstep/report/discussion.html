All figures quoted below are 5-run averages, in MOPS. The machine has 4 physical cores, so ideal speedup is bounded by 4. Every configuration above 4 threads is oversubscribed and relies on OS time-slicing, which is why every curve is flat from 4 to 16 threads. Across all 42 configurations no run collapsed under oversubscription, and the coefficient of variation stayed at or below about 12% (one outlier: lazy list, N=1000, read-only, at 18%).

6.1 Doubly linked list: lazy vs non-blocking

  • Scalability comes from traversal parallelism. A list operation spends almost all of its time walking about N/4 nodes, and walking is read-only, so threads only share cache lines in the Shared state. At N=10,000 both lists scale almost linearly up to the core count. At 4 threads the lazy list reaches 3.66× (update-only) and 3.81× (read-only), and the lock-free list reaches 3.49× and 3.70×. Conflicts on the same pair of nodes are rare in a list of about 5,000 nodes.
  • At N=1,000, lock contention limits the lazy list. Update-heavy speedup is only 1.60× (0C-50I-50D) and 1.69× (50C-25I-25D) at 4 threads, and peaks around 1.8×. With only about 500 nodes, two threads frequently need overlapping pred/curr locks. A thread that fails validation must re-traverse, and the lock words and marked flags of hot nodes bounce between cores (cache-coherence traffic). At 2 threads the lazy list gains nothing (1.35 → 1.32 MOPS).
  • The lock-free list scales better at N=1,000: 3.14× (0C-50I-50D) and 3.29× (50C-25I-25D) at 4 threads. A failed CAS costs only a local retry rather than a blocked thread, and no thread ever waits for a descheduled lock holder.
  • The lazy list has higher absolute throughput anyway. At N=1,000 and 16 threads it reaches 2.35 MOPS against 1.47 for the lock-free list (update-only), and 8.0 against 3.4 MOPS (read-only). Each step of the lock-free traversal goes through an AtomicMarkableReference: node → reference object → immutable (ref, mark) pair → next node. That is about three dependent loads per hop instead of one, and every successful CAS allocates a new pair. The lazy list follows plain volatile fields. In other words, the non-blocking version wins on scalability and lock-freedom but pays a constant synchronization overhead per node visited.
  • The effect of N is algorithmic. Throughput drops by roughly 15roughly 30–45×ndash;40roughly 15–40× from N=1,000 to N=10,000 (e.g. lazy list read-only, 4 threads: 7.0 → 0.19 MOPS), consistent with O(N) traversal and with the larger list no longer fitting in L1/L2 cache.

6.2 Stack: lazy lock-based vs Treiber

  • Update workloads do not scale for either stack. A stack has one hot spot (top) and serialises all updates. In the update-only workload at N=1,000, the Treiber stack falls from 51.1 MOPS at 1 thread to 14.3 at 2 and 9.6 at 4 (speedup 0.19). Every push and pop CASes the same cache line. Under contention most CASes fail (CAS failures and retries), and each attempt forces the line to move between cores in exclusive state, so throughput is set by cache-line transfer latency rather than by core count.
  • The lock-based stack degrades less: 19.5 MOPS at ≥4 threads, 2× the Treiber stack. Uncontended it is slower (30.7 vs 51.1 MOPS at 1 thread) because each operation pays lock acquire/release on top of the update. Under contention, however, the non-fair ReentrantLock lets a running thread re-acquire the lock several times in a row while its cache lines are still local, which effectively batches operations. In the Treiber stack, by contrast, every thread keeps attempting CAS and invalidating the line. The worst case for the lock is exactly 2 threads (8.4 MOPS), where hand-offs between the two threads are most frequent.
  • Read-only (peek) scales up to the core count: about 2.6× for Treiber and about 2.0× for LockStack at 4 threads, saturating near 1.45–1.56×109 ops/s. Read-only access keeps top in the Shared state in every core's cache, so no coherence traffic is generated. The sub-linear speedup reflects the per-operation harness cost (workload array, dispatch) and the fact that 1-thread peek is already only about 1.7 ns.
  • N has no effect on the stacks or queues (within noise), as expected for O(1) operations. N only sets the prefill depth.

6.3 Queue: two-lock vs Michael-Scott

  • The same hot-spot behaviour appears. In the update-only workload, MSQueue falls from 38.6 MOPS (1 thread) to 8.2 MOPS (4 threads, speedup 0.21), and LockQueue falls from 29.7 to 17.8 MOPS (0.60).
  • The two-lock queue wins under contention (about 18 vs 8–10 MOPS update-only, about 33 vs 17 MOPS for 50C-25I-25D). Enqueuers and dequeuers synchronise on different locks and different cache lines, so the two ends genuinely proceed in parallel. In MSQueue, by contrast, every enqueue performs two CASes (link, then swing tail), dequeuers also read tail, and threads help advance a lagging tail. All of this generates extra CAS failures and shared-memory contention on head, tail and the last node.
  • Uncontended, MSQueue is faster (38.6 vs 29.7 MOPS) because CAS is cheaper than lock + unlock. For read-only peek, LockQueue saturates higher (about 1,410 vs about 1,060 MOPS) because peek on a plain volatile next field needs one fewer dependent load than AtomicReference.get().
  • The GC-nepotism problem described in Section 2.6 is itself an HPC lesson. Before the fix, memory-management overhead dominated, and young GC pauses of more than 125 ms distorted every queue measurement.

6.4 Treiber vs elimination backoff stack

  • Elimination substantially reduces contention on the central stack. From 8 to 16 threads, the update-only workload gives 34–41 MOPS for the elimination stack against 9.4–10.3 MOPS for the Treiber stack, i.e. 3.3–4.4× higher throughput. For 50C-25I-25D, it gives 48–58 MOPS against 18–20 MOPS (about 2.5–3.1×). Results are almost identical for N=1,000 and N=10,000.
  • The mechanism: when a CAS on top fails, the thread moves to a random exchanger slot instead of retrying immediately. There, a push and a pop cancel out with two or three CASes on a cache line that only those two threads touch. This spreads the traffic across 8 lines instead of 1, and every elimination removes two operations from the central stack.
  • The benefit grows with the update fraction. Elimination needs complementary push/pop pairs, so 0C-50I-50D (50/50 push/pop) gains the most, while the read-only workload never fails a CAS and the two stacks are identical (about 1,540 MOPS).
  • Elimination does not make the stack scale: its speedup is still below 1 (0.63–0.71 at 4–16 threads), because a single core running without contention remains the fastest configuration for an inherently sequential object. What elimination achieves is much more graceful degradation under contention, which is exactly the regime (8–16 threads) the assignment highlights. At 1 thread there is no contention, the backoff path is never taken, and the two stacks perform the same (57 vs 51 MOPS, within run-to-run noise).

6.5 Summary

  • Speedup saturation at p = 4 appears everywhere and is set by the hardware (4 cores). Beyond that, thread scheduling just time-slices the same 4 cores, and throughput stays flat rather than collapsing.
  • Structures with distributed access (lists) scale close to linearly until saturation. Structures with a single hot spot (stacks, queues) show negative speedup under updates, caused by CAS failures and cache-coherence traffic, and only read-only workloads scale.
  • Lock-free is not automatically faster. It avoids blocking and scales better in the lists, but its higher synchronization overhead per step (atomic wrappers, retries, helping) can make it slower than a well-designed lock-based structure, as the lists, the stacks under contention and the queues all show. Contention-reduction techniques such as elimination (or the two-lock split of the queue) matter more than the choice between locks and CAS.
